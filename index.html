Understanding Relaxed Optimal Transport

L1feiyu

Classical optimal transport insists that every unit of supply is shipped and every unit of demand is met. Three well-known relaxations drop that insistence in three different ways. They are usually introduced separately, in separate papers, with separate algorithms. This article puts them on the same data, shows they are one geometry with four different projections, and argues that the one that matters most is the one that bounds distance rather than mass.

Optimal transport asks a concrete question: given a pile of mass arranged one way and a hole shaped another way, what is the cheapest way to move the first into the second? Write the source masses as a vector \(a\), the target masses as \(b\), and the cost of moving one unit from source \(i\) to target \(j\) as \(C_{ij}\). The Kantorovich problem looks for a transport plan \(P\), where \(P_{ij}\) is how much mass takes the route from \(i\) to \(j\):

$$ \min_{P \in U(a,b)} \langle P, C\rangle, \qquad U(a,b) = \{\, P \ge 0 \;:\; P\mathbf{1} = a,\;\; P^{\top}\mathbf{1} = b \,\}. $$

Those two equalities are the entire subject of this article. \(P\mathbf{1} = a\) says every source point ships all of its mass. \(P^{\top}\mathbf{1} = b\) says every target point receives exactly what it asked for. Together they make the feasible set a polytope of plans that all move the same total amount, and the only question left is which routes to use.

That is a strong assumption, and in practice it is frequently false. Your source has a bakery that bakes more than the town can eat. Your target has a café across the river that no bakery can reasonably serve. Your two tissue slices were cut a millimetre apart and one of them is torn. In each case the constraint is not a description of the problem — it is a bug, and it forces the plan to invent correspondences to satisfy an accounting rule.

Three ways to let go

Partial OT: name the mass

The most direct fix is to say outright that only part of the mass should move. Replace the equalities with inequalities, and pin the total:

$$ \begin{gathered} \min_{P \in U(\le a,\, \le b)} \langle P, C\rangle \quad\text{subject to}\quad \langle P, \mathbf{1}\rangle = s, \\[6pt] U(\le a,\, \le b) = \{\, P \ge 0 \;:\; P\mathbf{1} \le a,\;\; P^{\top}\mathbf{1} \le b \,\}. \end{gathered} $$

Now a source point may keep some or all of its mass, and a target point may go partly unfilled. The parameter \(s\) is the fraction of mass you are willing to move. This is the oldest of the three relaxations — it goes back to Caffarelli and McCann's work on the free-boundary problem — and it is the natural formulation when you genuinely know the overlap, for instance because you know what fraction of your sample is contaminated.

The catch is in that same sentence. \(s\) is a quantity, and you have to supply it. If you knew how much of your data was junk you would often not need the method.

Unbalanced OT: make the marginals negotiable

A softer approach is to keep both marginals as goals rather than constraints, and charge for missing them. Charging with a Kullback–Leibler divergence gives the unbalanced problem of Chizat, Peyré, Schmitzer and Vialard:

$$ \min_{P \ge 0}\; \langle P, C\rangle + \tau\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(P\mathbf{1} \,\|\, a\right) + \tau\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(P^{\top}\mathbf{1} \,\|\, b\right). $$

There is no hard constraint left at all. Small \(\tau\) means the marginals barely matter and almost nothing moves; large \(\tau\) recovers balanced OT in the limit. Because KL is smooth, mass responds smoothly to \(\tau\) — there are no jumps, which is exactly what you want if the relaxation sits inside a larger model you are differentiating through.

The cost of that smoothness is that \(\tau\) is a single dial controlling a soft trade-off with no natural units. It does not correspond to a quantity you can measure, and it interacts with \(\varepsilon\), with the scale of \(C\), and with the total mass all at once.

Supervised OT: forbid the routes, and let the mass follow

Supervised OT, introduced by Cang, Nie and Zhao, also relaxes the marginals — with an \(\ell^1\) penalty rather than a KL one, over plans that may undershoot:

$$ \min_{P \in U(\le a,\, \le b)}\; \langle P, C\rangle + \gamma\Big( \|a - P\mathbf{1}\|_1 + \|b - P^{\top}\mathbf{1}\|_1 \Big). $$

But \(\gamma\) is not where this method lives, and reading it as "the supervised OT knob" gets the whole thing backwards. In practice \(\gamma\) is a constant: normalise the cost matrix to \((0,1)\), set \(\gamma = 2\), and never touch it again.

The reason is a one-line calculation. Over plans with \(P\mathbf{1} \le a\), the \(\ell^1\) term is not really a norm — it is just the unmoved mass, \(\|a - P\mathbf{1}\|_1 = |a| - \langle P, \mathbf{1}\rangle\), and likewise for \(b\). So the objective collapses to \(\langle P,\, C - 2\gamma\rangle\) plus a constant, and a route is worth using exactly when \(C_{ij} < 2\gamma\). (The factor of two is because moving a unit relieves the penalty at both ends.) With every cost at most \(1\) and \(\gamma = 2\), that threshold sits at \(4\) — above everything in the matrix. The price never bites. Every route that is allowed gets used, and \(\gamma\) is chosen precisely to guarantee that: it is set large enough to get out of the way.

What decides the plan, then, is which routes are allowed at all. This is the part the name refers to: supervised OT lets you forbid a route outright by setting \(C_{ij} = \infty\), and the usual way to do that is a cutoff — everything costing more than some threshold is out. Mass whose only partners lie beyond the cutoff has nowhere permitted to go, so it stays.

So the knob is the cutoff, and it is denominated in the units of the cost matrix: a distance, a dissimilarity, an interaction radius. That is a categorically different thing to specify than \(s\) or \(\tau\). You are not saying how much mass should move, nor how badly you want the marginals — you are saying how far mass is allowed to travel, and the amount that moves is an output, not an input.

The cutoff need not be a distance threshold. Any rule that says which pairs may correspond works the same way — matching labels, compatible cell types, a time ordering that forbids going backwards. A threshold on \(C\) is just the most common form of it.

What the marginals actually do

The scatter plots above show where mass goes. They do not show the thing that separates these four methods, which is the gap between the marginals you asked for and the marginals you got. The coupling matrix does show it: pale bars are the request, solid bars are the delivery.

One geometry, four projections

Add an entropy term and every problem in this article becomes the same kind of object. Write \(K = e^{-C/\varepsilon}\) for the Gibbs kernel. Then entropic OT is a KL projection: find the plan closest to \(K\), in Kullback–Leibler divergence, that satisfies the constraints.

$$ \min_{P \in \mathcal{C}}\ \mathrm{KL}(P \,\|\, K), \qquad \mathrm{KL}(P \,\|\, K) = \sum_{ij} P_{ij} \log\frac{P_{ij}}{K_{ij}} - P_{ij} + K_{ij}. $$

This is the view of Benamou, Carlier, Cuturi, Nenna and Peyré, and it is worth adopting because KL projections onto marginal constraints are cheap and explicit. Projecting \(P\) onto \(\{P\mathbf{1} = a\}\) is a row scaling; onto \(\{P^{\top}\mathbf{1} = b\}\), a column scaling:

$$ \Pi_{\text{rows}}(P) = \mathrm{diag}\!\left(\frac{a}{P\mathbf{1}}\right) P, \qquad \Pi_{\text{cols}}(P) = P\,\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\frac{b}{P^{\top}\mathbf{1}}\right). $$

Sinkhorn's algorithm is nothing more than alternating these two projections. Both sets are affine, and for affine sets alternating Bregman projections converge to the projection onto the intersection with no bookkeeping at all. That is balanced OT.

The three relaxations each change what is being projected onto — or replace a constraint with a penalty, in which case the projection becomes a proximal step in the same geometry. Writing \(P = \mathrm{diag}(u)\,K\,\mathrm{diag}(v)\), the row update of each is one line:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{balanced:}\quad & u \leftarrow \frac{a}{Kv} \\[6pt] \text{partial:}\quad & u \leftarrow u \cdot \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{a}{P\mathbf{1}}\right) \quad\text{(+ Dykstra corrections)} \\[6pt] \text{unbalanced:}\quad & u \leftarrow \left(\frac{a}{Kv}\right)^{\tau/(\tau+\varepsilon)} \\[6pt] \text{supervised:}\quad & u \leftarrow \min\!\left(\frac{a}{Kv},\ e^{\gamma/\varepsilon}\right) \end{aligned} $$

Partial OT projects onto inequalities. The KL projection onto \(\{P\mathbf{1} \le a\}\) scales only the rows that overshoot, and only downward. Then the columns, likewise. Then onto \(\{\langle P,\mathbf{1}\rangle = s\}\), which is a single global rescale. These three sets are convex but not affine, and that changes the algorithm: plain alternating projection onto non-affine sets converges to some feasible point, but in general not to the projection. Dykstra's algorithm restores it by carrying a correction factor for each set and re-applying it before each projection. POT's entropic_partial_wasserstein does exactly this, and so does the solver behind these figures.

Unbalanced OT has no constraint to project onto. It pays \(\tau\,\mathrm{KL}(P\mathbf{1}\,\|\,a)\) instead, and the proximal step of that penalty in KL geometry is a fractional scaling. The exponent \(\tau/(\tau+\varepsilon)\) lies in \((0,1)\), so each step goes part of the way toward the full projection and stops. As \(\tau \to \infty\) the exponent goes to 1 and you recover balanced OT; as \(\tau \to 0\) it goes to 0 and nothing moves.

Supervised OT pays \(\gamma\,\|a - P\mathbf{1}\|_1\) over \(P\mathbf{1} \le a\). Its proximal step is the full projection, capped: a row is rescaled to its target unless doing so would need a factor beyond \(e^{\gamma/\varepsilon}\). With \(\gamma\) pinned at 2 that cap is astronomically large, and it binds only for a row whose permitted partners cannot take its mass.

The dual potentials \(f = \varepsilon \log u\) of the log-domain implementation are these same scalings seen through a logarithm; the "ceiling at \(\gamma\)" is the cap on \(u\).

What each knob controls

Three methods, three parameters. Sweep each one and watch two things: how much mass moves, and how far the plan reaches — the cost of the longest route it actually uses.

The bottom-right panel is the one to look at. Supervised OT's longest route is the cutoff: a hard bound on how far any unit of mass may travel, set in the units of the cost matrix. Nothing in the other two rows resembles it. Partial OT will use a route of any length if that is what meeting the mass quota takes; unbalanced OT's reach drifts upward with \(\tau\) as a side effect of a trade-off it is making about something else.

The staircase in the top-right panel is the same fact from the mass side. Raising the cutoff changes nothing until it crosses the cost of some group of routes, at which point that whole group becomes admissible at once; with the entropic term the steps are rounded, and they sharpen as \(\varepsilon\) falls. The curve flattens well before the cutoff reaches 1, because past the point where every source has some permitted partner, widening the cutoff admits nothing new.

Partial OT budgets mass. Supervised OT bounds distance.

It is tempting to read the previous two sections as saying partial and supervised OT are interchangeable — both leave mass behind, both live in \(U(\le a, \le b)\), and there is a clean identity connecting them when nothing is forbidden. Cang, Nie and Zhao write supervised OT as partial OT with one extra optimisation wrapped around it, a maximisation over the transported mass:

$$ \max_{\theta \,\in\, [\,0,\ \min(a^{\top}\mathbf{1},\, b^{\top}\mathbf{1})\,]}\; \min_{P \in U(\le a,\, \le b)} \Big\{\, \langle P, C\rangle \;:\; \langle P, \mathbf{1}\rangle = \theta \,\Big\}. $$

The inner problem is partial OT at a prescribed mass \(\theta\); the outer one hands \(\theta\) to the solver. And if \(C\) has no forbidden entries and the two sides carry equal mass, nothing stops \(\theta\) from climbing to the total, so the whole thing degenerates to balanced OT — the same saturation we met from the \(\gamma\) side.

But that is the case where supervised OT is not doing anything. The moment the cutoff is in force, the two methods are different objects, and the difference is not one of parameterisation:

Partial OT controls how much mass moves and says nothing about how far. Given a quota \(s\), it uses whatever routes it needs, including long ones.

Supervised OT controls how far mass may move and lets the amount follow. Given a cutoff, no unit of mass travels a route costing more than that, and the transported mass is whatever the permitted routes can carry.

This is why the readouts under every figure include longest route alongside mass, cost and slack. Set the supervised cutoff to some value, read off the mass it moves, then hand that same mass to partial OT as its quota. The two plans transport the same amount, and their total costs are usually within a few percent of each other. Their longest routes are not: partial OT's overshoots the cutoff, often by a wide margin. Transport cost is a sum, and a sum hides a handful of long routes among many short ones; the cutoff is a maximum, and a maximum forbids them.

There is a second, structural asymmetry. With forbidden routes in \(C\), partial OT at a prescribed \(\theta\) can have an empty feasible set — there may simply be no way to move that much mass along permitted routes. Supervised OT never has that problem, because \(\theta = 0\) is always feasible: at worst it moves nothing, and says so. Fix the mass first and you can pose a problem with no solution. Bound the distance and let the mass follow, and you cannot.

The same story in three dimensions

Everything so far has been flat, which flatters the cutoff: in two dimensions "close" and "far" are easy to see. Here is a helix — a curve where neighbouring turns pass close to each other in space — and a copy of it that has lost its top turn and picked up a stray clump. A small cutoff permits matches along the curve and refuses the short jump between turns; a larger one lets that jump through. Balanced OT has no such choice to make, so it stretches to serve the clump and crowds the missing turn's mass into wherever it will fit.

Choosing between them

UseWhenWhat you must supplyWatch out for
Balanced OT The two distributions really are two views of the same mass — same classes, same proportions, no contamination. Nothing. It will manufacture correspondences rather than report that none exist.
Partial OT You know the overlap: a contamination rate, a known outlier fraction, a subsampling ratio. A mass fraction \(s\). It bounds how much moves, not how far: to hit the quota it will use routes of any length.
Unbalanced OT The relaxation lives inside a larger differentiable pipeline, or you want mass to respond smoothly. A KL weight \(\tau\). \(\tau\) has no natural units and couples to \(\varepsilon\) and the scale of \(C\).
Supervised OT You can say how far mass may travel — an interaction radius, a label rule, a time ordering — and want that bound enforced exactly, with the amount moved following from it. A cutoff on the normalised cost. \(\gamma\) stays at 2. With nothing forbidden it collapses to balanced OT, because \(\gamma = 2\) saturates. The bound on distance is the method.
