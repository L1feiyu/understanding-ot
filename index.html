Understanding Relaxed Optimal Transport

Partial, unbalanced and supervised OT, side by side

Classical optimal transport insists that every unit of supply is shipped and every unit of demand is met. Three well-known relaxations drop that insistence in three different ways. They are usually introduced separately, in separate papers, with separate algorithms. This article puts them on the same data, and shows they are the same algorithm with one line changed.

Optimal transport asks a concrete question: given a pile of mass arranged one way and a hole shaped another way, what is the cheapest way to move the first into the second? Write the source masses as a vector a, the target masses as b, and the cost of moving a unit from source i to target j as C[i][j]. The Kantorovich problem looks for a transport plan P, where P[i][j] is how much mass takes the route from i to j:

minimise <P, C> over U(a, b) = { P ≥ 0 : P·1 = a, Pᵀ·1 = b }

Those two equalities are the entire subject of this article. P·1 = a says every source point ships all of its mass. Pᵀ·1 = b says every target point receives exactly what it asked for. Together they make the feasible set a polytope of plans that all move the same total amount, and the only question left is which routes to use.

That is a strong assumption, and in practice it is frequently false. Your source cloud has an outlier that corresponds to nothing. Your target has a whole cluster the source never sampled. Your two datasets are 70/30 and 30/70 splits of the same two classes, so matching the marginals exactly requires shipping mass between classes that have nothing to do with each other. In each case the constraint is not a description of the problem — it is a bug, and it forces the plan to invent correspondences to satisfy an accounting rule.

Three ways to let go

Partial OT: name the mass

The most direct fix is to say outright that only part of the mass should move. Replace the equalities with inequalities, and pin the total:

minimise <P, C> subject to P·1 ≤ a, Pᵀ·1 ≤ b, <P, 1> = s

Now a source point may keep some or all of its mass, and a target point may go partly unfilled. The parameter s is the fraction of mass you are willing to move. This is the oldest of the three relaxations — it goes back to Caffarelli and McCann's work on the free-boundary problem, and it is the natural formulation when you genuinely know the overlap, for instance because you know what fraction of your sample is contaminated.

The catch is in that same sentence. s is a quantity, and you have to supply it. If you knew how much of your data was junk you would often not need the method.

Unbalanced OT: make the marginals negotiable

A softer approach is to keep both marginals as goals rather than constraints, and charge for missing them. Charging with a Kullback–Leibler divergence gives the unbalanced problem of Chizat, Peyré, Schmitzer and Vialard:

minimise <P, C> + τ·KL(P·1 ‖ a) + τ·KL(Pᵀ·1 ‖ b)

There is no hard constraint left at all. Small τ means the marginals barely matter and almost nothing moves; large τ recovers balanced OT in the limit. Because KL is smooth, mass responds smoothly to τ — there are no jumps, which is exactly what you want if the relaxation sits inside a larger model you are differentiating through.

The cost of that smoothness is that τ is a single dial controlling a soft trade-off with no natural units. It does not correspond to a quantity you can measure, and it interacts with ε, with the scale of C, and with the total mass all at once.

Supervised OT: forbid the routes, and let the mass follow

Supervised OT, introduced by Cang, Nie and Zhao, also relaxes the marginals — with an ℓ¹ penalty rather than a KL one, over plans that may undershoot:

minimise <P, C> + γ·( ‖a − P·1‖₁ + ‖b − Pᵀ·1‖₁ ) over P·1 ≤ a, Pᵀ·1 ≤ b

But γ is not where this method lives, and reading it as "the supervised OT knob" gets the whole thing backwards. In practice γ is a constant: normalise the cost matrix to (0,1), set γ = 2, and never touch it again.

The reason is a one-line calculation we will do properly further down: a route is worth using exactly when C[i][j] < 2γ. With every cost at most 1 and γ = 2, that threshold sits at 4 — above everything in the matrix. The price never bites. Every route that is allowed gets used, and γ is chosen precisely to guarantee that: it is set large enough to get out of the way.

What decides the plan, then, is which routes are allowed at all. This is the part the name refers to: supervised OT lets you forbid a route outright by setting C[i][j] = ∞, and the usual way to do that is a cutoff — everything costing more than some threshold is out. Mass whose only partners lie beyond the cutoff has nowhere permitted to go, so it stays.

So the knob is the cutoff, and it is denominated in the units of the cost matrix: a distance, a dissimilarity, an interaction radius. That is a categorically different thing to specify than s or τ. You are not saying how much mass should move, nor how badly you want the marginals — you are saying how far mass is allowed to travel, and the amount that moves is an output, not an input.

The cutoff need not be a distance threshold. Any rule that says which pairs may correspond works the same way — matching labels, compatible cell types, a time ordering that forbids going backwards. A threshold on C is just the most common form of it.

What the marginals actually do

The scatter plots above show where mass goes. They do not show the thing that separates these four methods, which is the gap between the marginals you asked for and the marginals you got. The coupling matrix does show it: pale bars are the request, solid bars are the delivery.

One algorithm, four projections

Add an entropy term ε·H(P) to any of these problems and they all become strictly convex, solvable by Sinkhorn-style alternating updates on a pair of dual potentials f and g. Written in the log domain, the shared step is

corr = ε·log(a[i]) − ε·log( Σj exp( (f[i] + g[j] − C[i][j]) / ε ) )

and every one of the four methods then updates f[i] from corr. The only difference between them is what happens to that update:

balanced f[i] ← f[i] + corr unbalanced f[i] ← (τ/(τ+ε)) · ( f[i] + corr ) supervised f[i] ← min( f[i] + corr, γ ) partial f[i] ← min( f[i] + corr, γ ) with γ solved for, to hit mass s

Balanced OT applies no projection, which is why it enforces the marginal exactly. Unbalanced OT shrinks the potential toward zero, so it never quite gets there. Supervised OT clips it against a ceiling. That single min is the ℓ¹ penalty viewed from the dual side: a source point whose potential wants to climb above γ is a point for which moving the mass is not worth the price, so it keeps it.

How much mass each knob moves

The three parameters are not interchangeable, and the clearest way to see that is to sweep each one and plot the mass it moves.

That staircase in the third panel is worth pausing on. It is not a numerical artefact — it is the method working correctly. Raising the cutoff changes nothing until it crosses the cost of some group of routes, at which point that whole group becomes admissible at once. With the entropic term the steps are rounded off, and they sharpen as ε falls. Notice too that the curve flattens at the top well before the cutoff reaches 1: past the point where every source has some permitted partner, widening the cutoff adds nothing.

Partial and supervised OT are the same problem

The staircase is a hint that partial and supervised OT are more closely related than their definitions suggest, and in fact they are two parameterisations of one problem. The derivation is three lines. Because supervised OT optimises over plans with P·1 ≤ a, the ℓ¹ penalty is not really a norm — it is just the unmoved mass:

‖a − P·1‖₁ = |a| − <P, 1> and likewise for b <P, C> + γ(‖a − P·1‖₁ + ‖b − Pᵀ·1‖₁) = <P, C − 2γ> + γ(|a| + |b|)

The constant drops out, so supervised OT without any forbidden routes is exactly min <P, C − 2γ> over U(≤a, ≤b) — which is the Lagrangian of partial OT with multiplier 2γ on the mass constraint. Every γ corresponds to some s, and every attainable s to some γ. Solving one solves the other; the solvers in this repository exploit that directly, running partial OT by finding the γ that lands on the mass you asked for. That word "attainable" is doing real work, and we come back to it below.

This also explains the factor of two. Moving a unit of mass from i to j relieves you of the penalty at both ends — γ on the source side and γ on the target side — so a route is worth using exactly when C[i][j] < 2γ.

It is worth being careful about what this does and does not say. It says the ℓ¹ relaxation in supervised OT is not a new way of deciding how much mass moves — it is partial OT's mass budget, written as a price. And since γ is in practice pinned at the saturating value, nobody uses that reparameterisation at all: at γ = 2 the price is switched off by construction.

Which is the point. The relaxation is not the content of supervised OT. It is the precondition for the content — the thing that makes it legal to leave mass behind, so that forbidding a route is a coherent instruction rather than an infeasible one.

The same statement, from the other direction

Cang, Nie and Zhao put the relationship directly: supervised OT is partial OT with one extra optimisation wrapped around it — a maximisation over the transported mass itself.

maximise over θ ∈ [ 0, min(aᵀ·1, bᵀ·1) ] : minimise <P, C> over P ∈ U(≤a, ≤b) subject to <P, 1> = θ

Read it from the inside out. The inner problem is exactly partial OT at a prescribed mass θ. The outer one hands θ to the solver instead of to you. Two consequences follow, and together they bracket the method rather neatly.

With no forbidden routes and equal total mass, supervised OT degenerates to balanced OT. Nothing stops θ from climbing to the full mass, so the outer maximisation takes it there. That is the same saturation we met from the γ side, arriving by a different road: with nothing forbidden, there is no work left for the method to do that balanced OT was not already doing.

With forbidden routes, partial OT can stop being well posed — and supervised OT does not. Prescribe some θ and the feasible set may simply be empty: there may be no way to move that much mass using only permitted routes. Supervised OT never runs into this, because θ = 0 is always feasible. At worst it moves nothing, and it says so.

This is the sharpest version of the argument this section has been circling. It is not merely that a relaxed marginal makes bans enforceable; it is that letting the solver choose θ is what keeps the question answerable at all. Fix the mass first and you can pose a problem with no solution. Let the mass follow from what is permitted and you cannot.

What supervision actually buys

Which leaves the question of what is genuinely new in supervised OT, and the answer is the other half of the method: the forbidden routes. Relaxing the marginals is about how much mass moves. Supervision is about which pairs are allowed to exchange it, and no amount of tuning s or τ can express that.

The figure below is built so that geometry lies. There are three labelled classes on each side, positioned so that the nearest target cluster is usually the wrong class. Turn supervision on and the wrong-class routes are forbidden.

Notice what happens with Balanced selected and supervision on. The mass through forbidden pairs is not zero. It cannot be: the marginals must be satisfied, the only routes that satisfy them are banned, and an infinite cost loses to an infeasible problem. This is the concrete reason supervision needs a relaxed marginal to be meaningful at all — a hard ban and a hard marginal are usually contradictory, and something has to give.

Choosing between them

UseWhenWhat you must supplyWatch out for
Balanced OT The two distributions really are two views of the same mass — same classes, same proportions, no contamination. Nothing. It will manufacture correspondences rather than report that none exist.
Partial OT You know the overlap: a contamination rate, a known outlier fraction, a subsampling ratio. A mass fraction s. Guessing s wrongly is worse than not relaxing at all; the plan silently truncates.
Unbalanced OT The relaxation lives inside a larger differentiable pipeline, or you want mass to respond smoothly. A KL weight τ. τ has no natural units and couples to ε and the scale of C.
Supervised OT You can state which pairs are allowed to correspond — an interaction radius, a label rule, a time ordering — and would rather specify that than a mass budget. A cutoff on the normalised cost. γ stays at 2. With nothing forbidden it collapses to balanced OT, because γ = 2 saturates. The supervision is the method.

Notes on the implementation

Every figure here solves its problem live in your browser; nothing is precomputed. The solver is about two hundred lines of dependency-free JavaScript in src/lib/ot/solvers.js, written entirely in the log domain so that small ε does not overflow. Forbidden routes are encoded as a large finite cost rather than a true Infinity, which would poison the log-sum-exp.

It is worth being exact about provenance, because "validated against POT" and "uses POT" are different claims. For supervised OT the reference is the authors' own perform_sOT_log, used verbatim and unmodified. For the other three, reference/ot_reference.py is a re-implementation written to reproduce the POT library's algorithms — it does not import POT, so on its own it is evidence about the algorithm rather than about the library. A second script, reference/pot_reference.py, runs the actual library and compares, and can regenerate the golden fixtures from POT so the JavaScript is tested against genuine library output. The JavaScript itself is a port of these updates into one shared log-sum-exp kernel, not a translation of anyone's source.

The suite also pins the behaviour this article relies on: γ being saturated at 2 so that varying it changes nothing, mass rising monotonically with the cutoff, exactly zero mass crossing it, and complementary slackness — a source potential below the ceiling means that point's marginal is met exactly, and a potential resting on the ceiling means it is not.

Two things surfaced while building this that are worth passing on, because both are easy to get wrong in exactly the way we got them wrong.

The first is that entropic partial OT is a KL projection onto the intersection of three convex sets, and Dykstra's algorithm reaches it by carrying a correction factor for each one. Drop those corrections and the iteration still converges somewhere feasible — the marginals hold, the mass is right — but to a plan with a strictly larger entropic objective. POT's entropic_partial_wasserstein carries them, and so does the solver here; the two agree to about 1e-14. The uncorrected variant is kept only as the contrast that makes the corrections visible.

An earlier version of this article claimed the reverse — that POT's routine skipped the corrections and returned suboptimal plans. It was wrong, and the way it went wrong is the interesting part. The NumPy "reference" it was measured against was itself missing the corrections, so the comparison ran a correct solver against a broken stand-in and then reported the difference as a property of the library. Running against the real POT is what exposed it: one mass level agreed to 1e-15 while another was off by 15% — the signature of two different algorithms, not of a convergence problem. Validating against your own re-implementation of something proves nothing about the something.

The second is a measurement trap. When you compare two plans, rank them by the objective actually being minimised — <P, C> + ε·Σ(P log P − P) — and not by transport cost alone. A regularised solver is entitled to accept a slightly costlier plan in exchange for enough entropy, so <P, C> on its own can order two plans the wrong way round. It does exactly that on one of the cases in reference/validate.py, and taking that ordering at face value would have sent us chasing a bug that was not there.
